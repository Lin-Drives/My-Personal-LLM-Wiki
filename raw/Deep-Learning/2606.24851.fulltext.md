```text
Abstract
We introduce the Hartley Neural Operator (HNO), a real-valued counterpart of the Fourier
Neural Operator (FNO), which learns PDE solution operators using global spectral convolu-
tions. For real-valued solutions, Fourier coefficients are conjugate symmetric, so standard
FNO implementations retain a half-spectrum with complex-valued multipliers. HNO instead
uses the real Discrete Hartley Transform and one real multiplier per retained mode. Because
Hartley coefficients do not have the same conjugate-symmetry redundancy, HNO retains
twice as many spectral corners; the two operators are therefore isoparametric at equal
width and differ primarily in spectral basis. The central thesis is that the appropriate basis
is a property of the operator itself. Self-adjoint elliptic operators, such as Poisson and
biharmonic problems, have real, symmetric Green’s functions represented exactly by even
real Hartley multipliers, favoring HNO. Time-dependent operators carry phase through wave
propagation and transport in advection, Burgers, and Navier–Stokes, which an even real
multiplier cannot represent; FNO is therefore favored, with an advantage that increases with
phase content. The phaseless heat equation is the borderline case. Training both operators
with identical settings across seven PDEs, three initial-condition families, and periodic and
Dirichlet boundary conditions, we observe this ordering, consistent with the Green’s-function
analysis. The result is a predictive rule rather than a universal ranking: match the spectral
basis to the symmetry and phase structure of the solution operator.
1 Introduction
Why PDE solvers remain challenging.Partial differential equations (PDEs) govern fundamental
physical processes, including seismic-wave propagation, heat diffusion, and quantum evolution. Classical
numerical methods—including finite differences (LeVeque, 2007), finite elements (Johnson, 2009), and spectral
methods (Canuto et al., 2006)—have enabled major advances, but can face trade-offs in accuracy, stability, and
computational cost, particularly in complex, high-dimensional, or real-time settings. Parabolic equations such
as the heat equation smooth discontinuities and attenuate high frequencies, whereas hyperbolic equations such
as the wave equation preserve information but introduce dispersive errors in numerical approximations, with
consequences for seismology, acoustics, and electromagnetics. Nonlinear equations such as Burgers’ equation
can develop steep gradients and shocks that challenge fixed-basis representations, while the Navier–Stokes
equations involve vorticity transport and turbulent cascades across scales. Elliptic equations, including
Poisson and biharmonic problems, impose global coupling because their solutions depend on the entire
domain.
```

```text
Moreover, traditional solvers generally require a new computation for every initial condition or parameter
choice, making repeated solves expensive in uncertainty quantification, inverse problems, and real-time
forecasting (Pathak et al., 2022). Neural operators (Li et al., 2020b; Lu et al., 2021; Kovachki et al., 2023)
address this bottleneck by learning solution operators, amortizing computational cost across families of PDE
problems and enabling rapid inference after training.
Neural operators learn mappings directly between function spaces. Among them, the Fourier Neural Operator
(FNO) (Li et al., 2020b) has been particularly successful, parameterizing convolutional kernels in the
frequency domain using the Fast Fourier Transform. Its architecture lifts the input to a higher-dimensional
representation, applies truncated Fourier convolutions that retain selected frequency modes, and projects
the result to the output space. Because these convolutions are implemented spectrally, FNOs can generalize
across discretizations and resolutions. This approach is grounded in operator universal approximation theory
(Chen & Chen, 1995). Related architectures include the branch–trunk design of DeepONet (Lu et al., 2021)
and wavelet-based operators (Tripura & Chakraborty, 2022; Gupta et al., 2021), which provide improved
time–frequency localization for multiscale phenomena.
Despite the prevalence of real-valued PDE solutions, the suitability of complex-valued Fourier representations
as spectral bases has received comparatively little attention. For real fields, Fourier coefficients exhibit
conjugate symmetry, and standard FNO implementations typically retain only one half of the spectrum while
using complex-valued multipliers. We introduce the Hartley Neural Operator (HNO), which replaces the
FFT with the real discrete Hartley transform (Hartley, 1942; Bracewell, 1983). Hartley coefficients are real
and avoid the conjugate-symmetry redundancy of Fourier coefficients, allowing HNOs to retain all frequency
quadrants without complex arithmetic. For symmetric kernels, the Hartley convolution theorem reduces
convolution to an elementwise product. Consequently, an even real-valued Hartley multiplier can represent
the corresponding operator exactly; more general phase-carrying operators require the omitted coupling
between thekand−kmodes.
A systematic comparison across PDE classes, three initial-condition families, and both periodic and Dirichlet
boundary conditions, using identical training protocols for both operators, yields four main findings.
1. A real-valued counterpart to the FNO.HNO uses one real multiplier per retained mode and
requires no even–odd splitting. Because it retains twice as many spectral corners as the FNO, the two
models have the same number of real spectral parameters at equal width. This shared real-weight
parameterization follows Wong et al. (2023; 2025) and isolates the spectral basis as the primary
architectural difference.
2. A basis–operator alignment principle.Under standard assumptions, self-adjoint elliptic operators
have real, symmetric Green’s functions and belong to the subspace of even real Hartley multipliers.
They can therefore be represented exactly using one even real multiplier per mode. Conversely, this
subspace excludes operators with phase-carrying symbols (Appendix E).
3. An operator-phase ordering that predicts the empirical split.The benchmark results
exhibit a clear division: HNO performs best on elliptic operators, whereas FNO performs best
on time-dependent operators. This division follows a monotonic ordering in phase content. The
biharmonic operator, being the most self-adjoint, yields the largest HNO advantage; advection and
Burgers’ equation, which are the most transport-dominated cases, favor FNO most strongly; and the
phaseless heat equation lies near the boundary. The initial-condition family changes the magnitude
of these effects but not their direction.
4. Benchmarks and cost analysis.We provide Burgers’ and two-dimensional Navier–Stokes vorticity
benchmarks for Hartley-based operators, together with an arithmetic-cost analysis and radix-4 FHT
measurements. The results show that the additional wall-clock time arises from the emulated-DHT
backend rather than from an inherent cost of the Hartley transform (Appendix D).
The takeaway is a guideline rather than a universal ranking: use the real Hartley basis when the solution
operator is (approximately) self-adjoint and phase-free, as in many elliptic and diffusive problems; use the
complex Fourier basis when the operator involves substantial oscillation or transport.
```

```text
Comparison with other transforms.The Hartley transform occupies a conceptual position between

useful for transient and multiscale signals, but require a choice of mother wavelet and can be shift-variant.
The DCT provides strong energy compaction for smooth data, although discontinuities can produce blocking
artifacts. For smooth, periodic, real-valued PDE solutions, the Hartley transform therefore offers a natural
representation without complex arithmetic.
Across all six PDEs in our preliminary experiments, the Multiwavelet Transform operator (MWT) (Tripura &
Chakraborty, 2022; Gupta et al., 2021) consistently underperformed both FNO and HNO, with errors larger
by factors of2–4. This result is consistent with the broader observation that smooth, periodic solutions with
substantial global frequency content are often better represented by global spectral bases than by compactly
supported wavelets. Appendix B provides a detailed comparison of the Hartley transform with wavelet and
cosine alternatives.
Other recent studies have explored alternative spectral choices for neural operators. The Walsh–Hadamard
Neural Operator (Cavallazzi et al., 2025) uses rectangular wave functions suited to discontinuous coefficients,
whereas Convolutional Neural Operators (Raonić, 2023) avoid explicit spectral representations in favor of
bandlimited convolutions. Together, these approaches reinforce the view that spectral-basis selection is an
active architectural choice with meaningful performance implications.
In parallel, Wong et al. (2023; 2025) adapted Hartley-based neural operators to three-dimensional medical-
image segmentation. Their results show that replacing the FFT with the DHT enables real-valued frequency-
domain processing and achieves robust performance across resolutions with fewer than35k parameters.
Their model uses a single shared real-valued weight per frequency mode. Our HNO adopts the same
parameterization: one real multiplier per retained mode rather than separate even–odd weight pairs for each
quadrant. Our theoretical analysis, based on Green’s-function symmetry and operator phase content, explains
why Hartley-based operators perform well for some operators but not for others. Accordingly, our comparison
of FNO and HNO focuses on the central distinction between real and complex spectral representations.
```

```text
Data-driven neural-operator training.We train HNO using supervised input–output pairs generated
by numerical solvers. Because this approach learns the solution operator directly from examples, it avoids
differentiating through the PDE and the optimization difficulties associated with balancing multiple loss
terms (Wang et al., 2020). For time-dependent PDEs, we generate the ground truth with finite-difference
methods rather than spectral solvers, preventing the training data from embedding an implicit bias toward
Fourier representations. For elliptic PDEs such as Poisson and biharmonic equations, we instead use spectral
solvers because the neural operator learns a source-to-solution map rather than a time-evolution operator,
and the spectral solution is exact.
This setup isolates the effect of the spectral basis: performance differences between HNO and FNO primarily
reflect how well each transform matches the PDE structure, rather than artifacts of the data-generation
procedure.
Matched training for a basis-only comparison.Because HNO and FNO are isoparametric at equal
width, we train both using the same optimizer, learning rate, schedule, weight decay, gradient clipping, and
width. This protocol ensures that differences in accuracy are attributable to the spectral basis rather than to
hyperparameter tuning. An earlier overparameterized Hartley variant used separate even–odd weight pairs
for every frequency quadrant and appeared to require a separate training recipe. We attribute this behavior
to redundant parameterization. In contrast, the real-diagonal HNO trains stably under the FNO protocol,
making matched hyperparameters both fair and sufficient.
```

```text
5 Discussion
Our experiments suggest a single organizing principle: the symmetry and phase structure of the solution
operator should guide the choice of spectral basis. Real, self-adjoint, phase-free operators are naturally
matched to the real Hartley basis, whereas operators with substantial phase content are better matched to
the complex Fourier basis. The following subsections discuss the mechanisms underlying this division, its
computational implications, and its relationship to other real-valued operator representations.
5.1 Green’s-Function Alignment Explains Elliptic Dominance
HNO’s advantage on elliptic PDEs follows from the symmetry of their Green’s functions. Under the
assumptions of Theorem E.6, a self-adjoint elliptic operator has a real, symmetric Green’s function. In
translation-invariant settings, this corresponds to a real and even spectral symbol; the Fourier and Hartley
representations then encode the same operator. For symmetric kernels, Hartley convolution reduces to
elementwise multiplication (Corollary E.9), placing the target in HNO’s even real-multiplier subspace. An
unconstrained FNO instead uses complex-valued multipliers and therefore introduces additional imaginary
degrees of freedom (Proposition E.7) that are unnecessary for this operator class.
```

```text
The real-diagonal HNO layer uses one real matrix–vector multiplication per retained spectral mode and does
not require complex arithmetic. At the spectral-layer level, this gives a simpler parameterization and lower
per-mode arithmetic than a complex FNO multiplier. In the present implementation, however, the DHT is
evaluated as
Re{F[x]}−Im{F[x]}
using a full complexfftn. This implementation does not exploit the real-arithmetic structure of a native fast
Hartley transform and therefore incurs additional wall-clock cost relative to therfft backend used by FNO.
This overhead is a property of the emulated-DHT backend rather than an intrinsic limitation of the Hartley
transform. A native fast Hartley transform based on real arithmetic could substantially reduce this cost.
Indeed, the radix-4 FHT measurements in Appendix D, Table 1, indicate considerable optimization headroom,
with transform-level speedups of40–131×over the corresponding radix-2 implementation. Relative to the
accuracy differences induced by basis selection, the observed overhead remains modest.
5.4 Related Real-Basis Operators
Recent work on Walsh–Hadamard operators for discontinuous coefficients (Cavallazzi et al., 2025) and
convolutional neural operators (Raonić, 2023) further supports the view that basis selection is an active
architectural choice. The Walsh–Hadamard basis is suited to piecewise or discontinuous structure, whereas
convolutional neural operators avoid an explicit spectral basis altogether.
Independent evidence comes from the HNOSeg line of work (Wong et al., 2023; 2025). In three-dimensional
medical-image segmentation, Hartley-based operators achieve strong accuracy and resolution robustness
with fewer than35k parameters, substantially fewer than the parameter counts of conventional CNN- and
transformer-based alternatives. Their observation that real-valued frequency representations permit stacked
nonlinear operations complements our results. In our setting, the real-valued spectral diagonal exactly matches
real, symmetric solution operators, while its limitations become apparent for operators whose symbols carry
nonzero phase.

Dirichlet boundary conditions. These settings may not capture all effects that arise at higher resolutions,
```

```text
6 Conclusion
We introduced the Hartley Neural Operator (HNO), a real-valued counterpart to the Fourier Neural Operator
(FNO) that uses one real multiplier per retained mode and an isoparametric parameterization at equal width.
Our results support a principle for basis selection rather than a universal ranking of the two operators.
Self-adjoint elliptic operators with real, symmetric Green’s functions lie close to HNO’s even real-multiplier
subspace, giving HNO its largest advantage on problems such as the biharmonic equation. By contrast,
this subspace cannot represent nonzero phase without additionalk/−kcoupling, so FNO performs better
on oscillatory and transport-dominated operators. Across our benchmarks, the FNO advantage generally
increases from the phase-free heat equation through wave and transport-dominated problems. Initial-condition
families and boundary conditions primarily affect the magnitude of these differences rather than their direction.
The practical guideline is therefore to match the spectral basis to the symmetry and phase structure of
the solution operator. Real, even, phase-free operators are natural candidates for a Hartley representation,
whereas oscillatory and advective operators are better suited to a complex Fourier representation. Future work
should investigate native differentiable FHT kernels, data-driven per-mode basis selection, and comparisons
with nonspectral architectures such as DeepONet (Lu et al., 2021) and convolutional neural operators (Raonić,
2023).
```

```text
B Comparison with Wavelet and Cosine Transforms
Integral transforms, including the wavelet transform (Daubechies, 1988), the Hartley transform, and the
discrete cosine transform (DCT) (Ahmed et al., 1974), enable data analysis and representation in transformed
domains. In the wavelet case, basis functions are generated by dilating and translating a “mother wavelet”
(Akansu & Haddad, 1992), whereas the Hartley transform relies on cas functions (Definition A.2). The DCT,
built on cosine functions, excels at concentrating signal energy into a small number of coefficients—a trait
exploited in JPEG compression (Wallace, 1991)—yet it suffers from blocking artifacts at discontinuities and,
unlike the Hartley transform, is not self-inverse.
Wavelet and Hartley transforms both involve integrating the original signal against a kernel: the wavelet
function (Kaiser, 1994) for wavelets and the cas function for Hartley. Wavelets are particularly adept at
capturing transient events and multiscale phenomena, but they demand careful selection of a mother wavelet,
exhibit shift variance, and cannot be readily operated upon in the frequency domain. In contrast, the
Hartley transform’s real-valued formulation, which couples sinusoidal and cosinusoidal components, yields
representations well suited to smooth, periodic signals whose spectral content is globally distributed.
Empirically, the Multiwavelet Transform operator (MWT) (Tripura & Chakraborty, 2022; Gupta et al., 2021)
consistently underperformed both FNO and HNO by factors of 2–4×across all six PDEs tested. This outcome
supports the view that smooth, periodic solutions with global frequency structure are better captured by
spectral bases than by compactly supported wavelets.
```
